The Simple GDPR and Legal Checklist Every European Small Business Website Needs

Cookie banners, privacy pages, and footer details, in plain English, so your site looks professional and safe to work with.

Octavia Sirbu November 3, 2026

If you run a small business in Europe, whether you are a consultant in London, a clinic in Paris, a studio in Copenhagen, or an agency in Bucharest, your website has to do more than look good. It has to follow European privacy and transparency rules.

Many small business owners put off launching or updating their site because GDPR, cookie banners, and legal footers feel overwhelming. Getting these basics right does more than keep regulators happy. It signals to European customers that your business is legitimate, professional, and safe to work with.

Here is the plain-English checklist of what every European small business website actually needs.

1. A cookie banner that gives a real choice

If your website uses anything beyond strictly necessary technical cookies, such as Google Analytics, Meta or LinkedIn tracking pixels, or embedded YouTube videos, you need visitor consent before those scripts fire.

  • Equal prominence. Your banner must make “Reject all” just as easy to click as “Accept all”. Hiding the reject button inside three layers of settings is no longer acceptable under EU and UK enforcement.
  • No pre-ticked boxes. Analytics and marketing cookies must stay turned off until the user actively clicks accept.
  • Easy to change later. Include a small “Cookie settings” link in your footer so visitors can withdraw or update their choice anytime.

Tip: if you do not run ads and switch to a privacy-friendly, cookieless analytics tool like Plausible or Fathom, you can often skip intrusive tracking banners altogether.

2. A clear, human-readable privacy policy

Every website with a contact form, newsletter sign-up, or booking widget collects personal data: names, email addresses, IP addresses. Your footer needs a clearly linked privacy policy written in plain language, not copied-and-pasted corporate legalese.

Make sure it clearly answers five questions:

  1. Who you are. Your business name, address, and contact email.
  2. What data you collect and why. For example: “We collect your name and email via our contact form solely to reply to your inquiry.”
  3. Which third-party tools touch that data. List your website host, email provider, booking tool (like Calendly or Doctolib), or CRM.
  4. How long you keep it. Even a simple rule like “We delete inquiry emails after 12 months if we do not work together” shows accountability.
  5. How visitors can delete or access their data. Provide a direct email address where customers can request to view, edit, or erase their information.

3. Proper company identification in your footer

Across Europe, anonymous business websites are a major red flag, and in several countries, omitting your official company details is illegal.

France (mentions légales)

Mandatory for every professional site. You must display your full legal name or company name, business address, contact email or phone, registration number (SIREN/SIRET or RCS), VAT number if applicable, the name of the publication director (directeur de la publication), and the name, address, and phone number of your website hosting provider.

Denmark (CVR)

Danish businesses are expected to display their CVR number, company name, physical address, and contact details clearly in the footer or on the contact page.

Romania (date de identificare)

Commercial sites must display the company name (denumire firmă), CUI / CIF (tax ID), Trade Register number (nr. reg. com.), registered office address, and, if selling to consumers, links to ANPC (SAL / SOL) consumer protection notices.

United Kingdom

Limited companies (Ltd) must display their registered company name, company registration number, place of registration (for example England and Wales), and registered office address on their website.

4. Clean, unbundled contact and newsletter forms

If you have a contact form or newsletter sign-up box on your site, keep two simple rules in mind:

  • Do not auto-subscribe inquiries. Someone filling out a “Request a quote” form did not consent to join your monthly marketing newsletter. If you want to add them to a mailing list, include a separate, unchecked opt-in box.
  • Only ask for what you need. Under GDPR’s data minimisation principle, do not ask for a phone number, home address, and company size on a simple contact form unless you genuinely need them to reply. Shorter forms also convert better.

5. HTTPS security (the padlock icon)

This is a basic technical requirement, yet plenty of older small business sites still miss it. Your entire website must run over HTTPS with an active SSL certificate so any data submitted through your forms is encrypted in transit. Modern browsers flag non-HTTPS sites with a “Not secure” warning that scares customers away immediately.

Building trust without ruining your design

The biggest mistake small businesses make is slapping a giant, ugly grey cookie wall across the entire mobile screen, ruining the first impression they worked so hard to create.

Good web design handles compliance cleanly and respectfully: fast cookieless analytics where possible, a sleek, non-intrusive consent bar when needed, and a well-organised footer that reassures European buyers they are dealing with a real, trustworthy business.
